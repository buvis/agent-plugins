# Philosophical lenses

Loaded at Tier 3, on top of the checklist and the techniques. They catch failure modes engineering checklists miss: substance confused with cause, names drifting from what they denote, testimony posing as evidence, a perspective mistaken for a universal truth, a false dichotomy dressed as a forced choice, false confidence under balanced evidence, and harm done to a working system in the act of changing it.

- [SR-RVD-092] Aristotle's four causes: probe the material cause (substrate, dependencies, third-party services, data shape; is it fit for the load and the longevity), the formal cause (architecture, interfaces, data model; coherent and minimal), the efficient cause (who builds and operates, with what process and budget; honest about cost), and the final cause (the user or business outcome; stated in one sentence, with every component tracing to it). A design that cannot state its final cause in one sentence solves the wrong problem, or none.
- [SR-RVD-093] Aristotle's mean: for each major choice a virtue lies between an excess and a deficiency (monolithic mud against micro-fragmentation; a silent system against a log firehose; hardcoded against over-generic; trust everything against validate everywhere; no retry against infinite retries). The design names both extremes for each major decision to show the mean was chosen, not stumbled into.
- [SR-RVD-094] Confucian rectification of names: for every key term demand one meaning within the design, a name that denotes what the thing is rather than what it is wished to be, a distinction from related terms (service, module, component, system), and honesty over euphemism; sloppy naming leaks into every test plan and incident review, and an author's resistance to a glossary is itself the finding.
- [SR-RVD-095] Nyaya pramanas: label the basis of each load-bearing claim as perception (measured in this or a representative system), inference (derived from established facts), analogy (a similar system behaves so), or testimony (an expert, a vendor, a blog, an industry consensus says so). Flag every load-bearing claim resting only on testimony or analogy, the weakest bases.
- [SR-RVD-096] Jain anekantavada: every absolute claim is true from a perspective; prefix it with "from the viewpoint of X" and test it from the others ("simple" for the API consumer and not the operator; "fast" at the median and not the tail; "secure" against outsiders and not insiders). A robust design qualifies the claim or shows it holds across every relevant perspective.
- [SR-RVD-097] Buddhist tetralemma: for each binary decision force the four corners, A, not A, both (a hybrid, a parallel run, a layered approach), and neither (the question is wrongly framed); many engineering dichotomies are smuggled framings, and the neither corner, most often missed, is the most fertile.
- [SR-RVD-098] Pyrrhonian epoché: when the arguments on each side are of about equal strength, the disciplined response is to suspend judgment, not to choose under a pretence of certainty. For each major decision ask whether the evidence supports the confidence expressed, whether an equipollent case is acknowledged and deferred through an experiment, a parallel run, or a flag, and whether the commitment is driven by a deadline rather than evidence; the fix is usually "instrument and defer", not "decide harder".
- [SR-RVD-099] Hippocratic do-no-harm: before a change, enumerate what works today that the change could break, degrade, or make harder: existing behaviour, workflows people rely on, downstream consumers' assumptions, invariants of the current system. The design keeps a "what we might harm" list beside "what we deliver"; a new benefit that quietly breaks five existing ones is a net loss.
