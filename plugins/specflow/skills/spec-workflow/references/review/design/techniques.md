# Advanced review techniques

The premortem is loaded at every tier; the rest at Tier 2 and Tier 3, and the persona walkthrough and the counterfactual constraints in full at Tier 3. Apply them beside the checklist: they catch systemic issues a section-by-section pass misses.

## Probing reasoning

- [SR-RVD-070] For a load-bearing claim, walk back through the reasoning instead of asserting that the conclusion is wrong, with the move that matches the gap: Socratic questioning when you suspect a gap but are not certain the author missed something; the five-whys descent when a claim feels load-bearing and you cannot tell whether it rests on evidence or habit; the claim ladder when the design uses compressed qualifiers without defining them.
- [SR-RVD-071] Socratic questioning has six kinds, each with a design bite: clarification ("what exactly do you mean by X; a concrete example"); assumption probes ("what are you assuming; how would we know it is wrong"); evidence probes ("what is the evidence; measured or intuition"); viewpoint probes ("the strongest argument against this; why not the obvious alternative"); implication probes ("if this is true, what else must be true; what does it commit us to in two years"); meta-questions ("why does this section matter; what are we avoiding"). A well-formed question yields information either way; assert only when you hold evidence.
- [SR-RVD-072] The five-whys descent asks "why" of a load-bearing claim until the chain ends at bedrock (a real constraint, validated evidence, an external commitment), an unvalidated assumption (flag, ask for validation), an inherited belief (flag, ask whether it still applies), a habit or preference (flag), or deference to authority (flag, ask whether the analogy holds at this scale). A chain that bottoms at "that is how we do it" leaves the assumption unmoored, to be re-derived from current constraints.
- [SR-RVD-073] The claim ladder takes a compressed assertion ("scalable", "resilient", "secure", "fast", "modular", "robust") and ascends: what concrete behaviour would be observed in a system that is X; what measurement distinguishes yes from no; what the design commits to on that measurement. An assertion that does not survive the ladder is a vocabulary placeholder, not a design decision.

## Techniques

- [SR-RVD-074] Premortem: imagine that the project failed eighteen months from now and write a four-to-six-sentence failure obituary naming the most plausible failure mode, the one the design waves away rather than the one it anticipates; then check whether the design mitigates it or only acknowledges it. For a six-month horizon run the regret interview instead: what would most likely be regretted at the first post-mortem.
- [SR-RVD-075] Inverse problem: reverse-engineer from the architecture the problem the design is actually optimised for, and compare it with the stated problem; designs get optimised for team preference, future flexibility, vendor relationships, or prestige rather than the named need.
- [SR-RVD-076] Cognitive bias scan, with a citation wherever one is suspected: sunk cost (chosen because someone already started building it); confirmation (only alternatives the author wanted to reject); availability (familiar technology without weighing the unfamiliar); authority ("a large company uses it" without proving applicability); anchoring (a timeline anchored to an imposed deadline); planning fallacy (no buffer for unknowns); curse of knowledge (context only the author has); the IKEA effect (an author too attached to evaluate alternatives); bikeshedding (disproportionate detail on a low-stakes decision).
- [SR-RVD-077] Negative space audit: catalogue what a competent design would address and this one does not, since the silences hide the worst problems; check per-dependency failure modes, operating burden, vendor exit, cost at projected scale, the data deletion path, time zones and locales, and the boundary cases (empty, maximum, negative, unicode, very long, very many).
- [SR-RVD-078] Conway's law check: map the component boundaries against the team boundaries and ask whether the architecture solves a coordination problem rather than a technical one, and whether that is intentional and acknowledged.
- [SR-RVD-079] Hidden coupling map: two components drawn apart can still be coupled by a shared table, a shared library version, a shared deployment cadence, a shared on-call rotation, a shared kill switch, a shared quota, a shared release train, or a shared incident response; list every implicit coupling and ask whether it is intentional.
- [SR-RVD-080] Falsifiability check: for every claim, ask what observation would prove it wrong; "highly available" is unfalsifiable, "99.95% monthly uptime measured by external probes" is not. Flag every unfalsifiable claim posing as a commitment, and demand a methodology for numerical precision without one, or accept an uncertainty band.
- [SR-RVD-081] Compression test: name the load-bearing tenth of the design, what survives when it is compressed to one page; when most of the text is not load-bearing, the design is still exploring, not deciding. The telephone variant: would a two-page summary lead another team to build the same system; when not, the design lives in tacit context.
- [SR-RVD-082] Section weight audit: for each top-level section, measure its weight (words, or bullets when the design is mostly bulleted), take the median across sections, and ask of a heavy outlier (over three times the median) whether its importance justifies its depth, and of a light outlier (under a third) whether the topic is low-stakes or rushed; many outliers, or a critical section as a light outlier, are a finding. Audit a container section (several parallel sub-sections of comparable depth) at the sub-section level, since balance is a per-level property; strip fenced code before counting, since a heading inside a fence is content, not structure. Run it even when the design reads smoothly; `scripts/review/section_weight_audit.py` does the counting.
- [SR-RVD-083] Surprise check: find where the design says something that could not be predicted from the title and the problem statement; that is where the real design lives. A design with no surprise is description, not decision, and the finding asks the author what was considered and rejected.
- [SR-RVD-084] Persona walkthrough: read the design as each of seven readers and keep the findings unique to that view: a new hire on day one (can a mental model be built from this alone); the on-call engineer at three in the morning (can the failing component and a runbook be found); a security auditor (the threat model, the controls, the audit trail); a finance or capacity planner (cost and resource forecast); a regulator or compliance officer where applicable (evidence of required controls); a customer or end user (what changes, when, and how to recover from a bad change); an adversary (how to exploit, abuse, or break it).
- [SR-RVD-085] Time-horizon walkthrough: day one (is the launch feasible, observable, and safely reversible); day one hundred (does it run without daily human intervention); day one thousand (lethal debt, or graceful ageing); end of life (how it dies without stranding users, data, or consumers).
- [SR-RVD-086] Counterfactual constraints: change the constraints and see which decisions are fragile (those the constraint drove) and which are robust: half the headcount; a tenth of the budget; no new technology, only what is in house; twice the deadline; one region instead of several; no vendor at all; vendor-only, build nothing.
- [SR-RVD-087] Load-bearing assumption graph: list every assumption the design depends on, mark which would cascade and kill the project if wrong, and classify each load-bearing one as validated by data, by a prototype, by an external commitment, or merely plausible; the plausible-only load-bearing ones are the largest risks.
- [SR-RVD-088] Bus factor per component: how many people can operate, debug, and extend each component; a bus factor of one is a risk even when nobody is leaving, above all when the one is the design's author.
- [SR-RVD-089] Asymmetric risk audit: find the decisions whose downside is huge relative to the upside, or the reverse (a cheap feature-flag system that enables safe rollouts); lean into asymmetric upside and away from asymmetric downside, and flag any asymmetric-downside bet the design has not surfaced as one.
- [SR-RVD-090] Decision quality versus outcome quality: judge each decision on the information available now, not on an imagined outcome; critique the reasoning, and flag decisions justified only by a past lucky outcome.
- [SR-RVD-091] What if we are wrong about the problem itself: the worst failure is solving the wrong problem perfectly, so ask whether the problem statement is questioned anywhere and grounded in interviews, data, or observation. A design whose problem is assumed rather than evidenced is a finding, and when the problem itself is wrong the finding goes back to the requirements through an explicit upstream edit, which stales the design.
